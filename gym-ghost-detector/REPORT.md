## 0. Assignment Requirements (what this assignment is actually testing)

This assignment requires walking through a **complete supervised-learning classification workflow**, specifically:

1. Use **Claude-generated synthetic data** (6,000 rows, with features such as tenure, monthly fee, contract type, etc.);
2. Perform a **train/test split**;
3. Train and compare **three different classification models**;
4. Do **hyperparameter tuning with GridSearchCV**;
5. Evaluate with metrics such as **accuracy / precision / recall / ROC-AUC**;
6. Analyze **feature importance**;
7. Provide **interpretation and business recommendations**.

### Why require comparing three models?

This is the core point of the assignment, for three reasons:

- **There is no "one model to rule them all"** (No Free Lunch theorem): no algorithm is best on all datasets. Only by comparing them **on the same data and under the same evaluation standard** can you know which one fits the current problem.
- **It tests the "complexity vs. payoff" trade-off**: the three models range from simple (logistic regression) to complex (random forest, SVM). Comparing them answers a key question — **does a more complex model actually deliver better results?** If not, you should choose the simpler, more interpretable one.
- **It validates the robustness of the conclusion**: if three models with completely different underlying principles all point to the same conclusion (e.g., "contract type is the number-one churn driver"), then that conclusion is highly credible and not a fluke of any single model.

### What problem does this project actually solve?

**Business problem**: A gym's biggest pain point is members canceling their memberships (churn). Retaining an existing member costs far less than acquiring a new one. If we can **predict in advance which members are about to churn**, we can intervene proactively before they cancel (send offers, personal-trainer follow-ups, push long-term contracts), and thereby preserve revenue.

**Technical problem**: This is a **binary classification** problem — given a member's features, predict whether they will "churn (Churn=1)" or "stay (Churn=0)".

## 3. Steps (the full workflow)

The script `train_and_evaluate.py` runs the following 5 steps (it prints STEP 1/5 ~ 5/5 at runtime):

```
STEP 1  Load data + train/test split      (80/20, stratified, seed=42)
STEP 2  Train + GridSearchCV tuning        (three models)
STEP 3  Evaluate on the test set           (acc/precision/recall/F1/AUC)
STEP 4  Generate figures                   (ROC curves + feature importance)
STEP 5  Export metrics                      (metrics_summary.json)
```

### 3.1 Train/test split
- 80% train / 20% test;
- **stratify (stratified sampling)**: ensures the churn ratio is ~27% in both the training and test sets, avoiding bias introduced by the split.

### 3.2 Preprocessing
The data mixes numeric and categorical columns, so we use a `ColumnTransformer` to handle them uniformly:
- **Numeric columns → StandardScaler**: standardize to "mean 0, standard deviation 1", removing scale differences (e.g., age and distance are on very different scales);
- **Categorical columns → OneHotEncoder**: turn text into 0/1 columns (e.g., `contract_type` is split into 3 columns).

> Preprocessing is wrapped inside a **Pipeline** and is **fit separately within each cross-validation fold**, acting only on the training copy and **never modifying the original CSV** — this prevents "data leakage" (contaminating training with test-set information).
> After preprocessing, the number of features goes from **12 → 17**.

## 4. Training and Models

### 4.1 What the three models are and what each is for

| Model | Principle | Role in this project |
|------|------|------------------|
| **Logistic Regression** | Linear model; learns a weight for each feature, sums them, and passes through sigmoid to get a probability | **Baseline + interpretability workhorse.** Simple and fast; the coefficients directly tell you "how much each factor pushes churn odds up/down" |
| **Random Forest** | An ensemble model where many decision trees vote | **Nonlinearity + feature-importance workhorse.** Captures feature interactions and nonlinear relationships, and comes with built-in feature importance |
| **SVM (RBF kernel)** | Uses a kernel function to map data into a high-dimensional space and find the optimal separating surface | **Complex nonlinear boundary workhorse.** In theory can fit very convoluted decision boundaries |

### 4.2 First, get this straight: parameters vs. hyperparameters

There are two kinds of "numbers" in a model — don't confuse them:

| | **Parameters** | **Hyperparameters** |
|--|----------------------|------------------------------|
| Who sets them | The model **learns them during training** | **You set them manually before** training |
| Examples | Logistic regression's weights `w` and bias `b`; each split point in a tree | Logistic regression's `C`; random forest's `n_estimators`, `max_depth`; SVM's `C`, `gamma` |
| Do you manage them | No, `fit()` computes them automatically | Yes — this is exactly what "tuning" searches for |

> In one sentence: **parameters are the "knowledge the model learns," hyperparameters are the "learning rules you set for the model."** GridSearchCV in Section 4.5 tunes the hyperparameters.

### 4.3 The math and formulas behind the three models

#### ① Logistic Regression

**Idea**: multiply each feature by a weight and sum them, then use the sigmoid function to squash the result into a probability between 0 and 1.

Step 1 (linear combination):

$$z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_n x_n + b = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b$$

Step 2 (convert to a probability with sigmoid):

$$p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

Decision: $p \ge 0.5 \Rightarrow$ predict churn (1); $\; p < 0.5 \Rightarrow$ predict stay (0).

- $\mathbf{w}$ (weights) and $b$ (bias) are **parameters learned during training**. $w_i > 0$ → that feature pushes churn up; $w_i < 0$ → it lowers churn. This is why Section 5.3 can interpret the coefficients.
- Training objective: minimize the **log loss**, plus a regularization term to prevent overfitting:

$$\text{Loss} = -\sum_i \big[\, y_i \log(p_i) + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) \,\big] + \frac{1}{C}\lVert \mathbf{w}\rVert^2$$

  Here $C$ (a hyperparameter) controls regularization strength: larger $C$ → weaker regularization → fits the training data more closely.
- **Characteristics**: the decision boundary is **a straight line/plane** (linear), so it is simple, fast, and interpretable, but cannot capture complex nonlinear relationships.

#### ② Random Forest

**Idea**: train many **decision trees**, each looking at part of the data, then **vote/average** at the end.

- A single **decision tree** repeatedly asks "yes/no questions" to split the data (e.g., "Is the contract monthly?" "Are weekly visits < 2?"), choosing the split that makes the subsets "purest." Purity is commonly measured with **Gini impurity**:

$$\text{Gini} = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2 \qquad (p_k = \text{the proportion of class } k \text{ at this node; closer to 0 = purer})$$

- **Random forest = an ensemble of many trees (Bagging)**: each tree is trained on **randomly sampled data + a randomly selected subset of features**, ensuring diversity among the trees; the final prediction **averages** all tree outputs:

$$P(\text{churn}) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{tree}_t(\mathbf{x}) \qquad (T = \text{number of trees} = \texttt{n\_estimators})$$
- Relevant **hyperparameters**: `n_estimators` (number of trees T), `max_depth` (max depth of each tree), `min_samples_split` (minimum samples a node needs before it can split further).
- **Characteristics**: automatically captures **nonlinearity and feature interactions**; comes with **feature importance** (how much a feature reduces impurity on average when used for splitting). The downside is it is less intuitive than logistic regression.

#### ③ SVM (Support Vector Machine, RBF kernel)

**Idea**: find the separating line with the **maximum margin**; when the data is not linearly separable, use a **kernel function** to map it into a higher-dimensional space and separate it there.

- Objective: find the separating surface $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ that maximizes the "margin" between the two classes, while allowing a few misclassifications (soft margin):

$$\min_{\mathbf{w},\,b}\;\; \frac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \qquad (\xi_i = \text{the degree of misclassification for sample } i)$$

  $C$ (a hyperparameter): the strength of the penalty for misclassification. Large $C$ → try hard not to make mistakes, more complex boundary; small $C$ → tolerate misclassification, smoother boundary.
- **RBF kernel (Gaussian kernel)**: measures how "alike" two samples are, implicitly mapping data into an infinite-dimensional space so it can draw **curved decision boundaries**:

$$K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp\!\big(-\gamma\,\lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\rVert^2\big)$$

  $\gamma$ (a hyperparameter): the range of influence of a single sample. Large $\gamma$ → small range of influence, very curvy boundary (prone to overfitting); small $\gamma$ → smoother boundary.
- The final decision is determined by the "support vectors" (the key samples closest to the boundary):

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sign}\!\Big(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}) + b\Big)$$
- **Characteristics**: can fit very complex boundaries, but trains slowly and is a **black box** (hard to interpret), and is very sensitive to `gamma`/`C` (which is why it gains the most from tuning in Section 4.5.4).

#### One-sentence comparison of the three formulas

| Model | Core formula (intuition) |
|------|----------------|
| Logistic Regression | $p = \sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b)$ —— weighted sum → squashed into a probability (**a straight line**) |
| Random Forest | $P = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \text{tree}_t(\mathbf{x})$ —— many trees vote (**a bunch of yes/no questions**) |
| SVM (RBF) | $f = \operatorname{sign}\!\big(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x})+b\big)$ —— maximum margin + kernel mapping (**curved boundary**) |

### 4.4 Key differences between the three models

| Dimension | Logistic Regression | Random Forest | SVM (RBF) |
|------|---------|---------|-----------|
| Handles nonlinearity? | ❌ Linear only | ✅ Yes | ✅ Yes |
| Interpretability | ⭐⭐⭐ Very strong (read coefficients) | ⭐⭐ Medium (read importances) | ⭐ Weak (black box) |
| Training speed | Fast | Medium | Slow (slower still on large data) |
| Needs standardization? | Yes | Not sensitive | **Strongly required** |
| Outputs probabilities | Natively | Can output | Needs extra calibration |

In one sentence: **logistic regression = a simple, transparent ruler; random forest = catches complex patterns and can explain its reasons; SVM = draws complex boundaries but acts like a black box.**

### 4.5 Hyperparameter tuning (GridSearchCV)

For each model we specify a set of candidate parameters and use **`GridSearchCV`** + **5-fold cross-validation**, with **ROC-AUC** as the scoring metric, to automatically try every combination and pick the best.
> This satisfies the assignment requirement: *"Uses scikit-learn's GridSearchCV or RandomizedSearchCV for basic hyperparameter tuning"* — this project uses **GridSearchCV** (one of the two).

#### 4.5.1 What each hyperparameter means

| Model | Hyperparameter | Meaning | Effect of increasing it |
|------|--------|------|-----------|
| Logistic Regression | `C` | The **inverse** of regularization strength (larger C = weaker regularization) | Large C → fits training data more, may overfit |
| Random Forest | `n_estimators` | **Number of trees** in the forest | More = more stable, but slower |
| | `max_depth` | **Max depth** of each tree (None = unlimited) | Deeper = can memorize detail, may overfit |
| | `min_samples_split` | **Minimum samples a node needs** to keep splitting | Larger → simpler trees, prevents overfitting |
| SVM (RBF) | `C` | The **penalty strength** for misclassification | Large C → try hard not to err, more complex boundary |
| | `gamma` | The **range of influence** of the RBF kernel (how far a single sample reaches) | Large gamma → curvier boundary, may overfit |

#### 4.5.2 Search space vs. finally selected value (three-model comparison)

| Model | Search space (candidate grid) | # Combos | **Best chosen by GridSearchCV** |
|------|---------------------------|:---:|-----------------------------|
| **Logistic Regression** | `C` ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10} | 4 | `C = 10` |
| **Random Forest** | `n_estimators`∈{200, 400}<br>`max_depth`∈{None, 6, 10}<br>`min_samples_split`∈{2, 5} | 2×3×2 = 12 | `n_estimators = 400`<br>`max_depth = 6`<br>`min_samples_split = 2` |
| **SVM (RBF)** | `C`∈{0.1, 1, 10}<br>`gamma`∈{scale, 0.01, 0.1} | 3×3 = 9 | `C = 1`<br>`gamma = 0.01` |

> Every combination of every model runs 5-fold CV, so the actual number of training runs = #combos × 5 (e.g., random forest = 12 × 5 = 60 runs). The program does this automatically and takes the combination with the highest average AUC.

#### 4.5.3 What the selected values tell us

- **Logistic Regression `C=10`** (very weak regularization): indicates the signal in the data is clear and doesn't need much suppression — letting the model fit fully works better.
- **Random Forest `max_depth=6`** (chose shallower trees rather than unlimited depth): shows that **limiting complexity prevents overfitting**; deeper trees weren't better — confirming that "the patterns in this data are not complex."
- **SVM `gamma=0.01`** (relatively small, smoother boundary): likewise shows that **a very curvy decision boundary isn't needed**; a smooth boundary generalizes better.
- **Common signal**: after tuning, all three models favor "simpler" configurations, consistently pointing to — **the patterns in the data are mostly linear/simple**, which also explains why the simplest logistic regression ultimately doesn't lose.

#### 4.5.4 Before vs. after tuning (quantifying the gains from tuning)

To prove tuning isn't just for show, here we compare **training directly with default parameters** vs. **after GridSearchCV tuning** (on the test set):

| Model | Default AUC | Tuned AUC | **AUC gain** | Default Acc | Tuned Acc |
|------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Logistic Regression | 0.813 | 0.813 | ≈ 0 (already optimal) | 0.771 | 0.771 |
| Random Forest | 0.799 | 0.809 | **+0.010** | 0.767 | 0.765 |
| **SVM (RBF)** | 0.771 | 0.811 | **+0.040** ⬆️ | 0.768 | 0.762 |

**Conclusions:**
- **SVM gains the most (AUC +0.040)**: the default `gamma` was a poor fit; tuning pulled it from "worst performer" up to "on par with the others." → SVM is **the most sensitive to hyperparameters** and most dependent on tuning.
- **Random forest gains a little (+0.010)**: limiting `max_depth=6` slightly improved generalization.
- **Logistic regression barely changes**: it is inherently simple and its default parameters are already near optimal, so there's little room to tune.
- **In one sentence**: tuning is more valuable for more complex, more sensitive models (SVM); for simple models (logistic regression) it adds only marginal gains. This is exactly the point that "basic hyperparameter tuning" is meant to demonstrate.

> **Why use ROC-AUC instead of accuracy to select models?** Because the data is imbalanced (27% churn). If a model predicts everyone as "no churn," its accuracy is still 73% but it's useless. AUC measures the "model's ability to rank/distinguish churners from non-churners," which is fairer under imbalance.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Three-model comparison (test set, 20% = 1,200 people)

| Model | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1 | **ROC-AUC** |
|------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **Logistic Regression** | 0.771 | 0.621 | 0.422 | **0.503** | **0.813** ✅ |
| Random Forest | 0.765 | 0.633 | 0.340 | 0.443 | 0.809 |
| SVM (RBF) | 0.762 | 0.629 | 0.319 | 0.423 | 0.811 |

**Quick reference for what the metrics mean:**
- **Accuracy** — the proportion of overall correct predictions
- **Precision** — of those predicted to "churn," the proportion who actually churn (how precise)
- **Recall** — of those who actually churn, the proportion successfully caught (how much was missed)
- **F1** — the harmonic mean of precision and recall (a balanced overall measure)
- **ROC-AUC** — the model's ranking/discrimination ability (the primary metric; closer to 1 is better)

### 5.2 Comparison conclusions

1. **The three models are nearly tied**: AUC is between 0.809–0.813, a gap of less than 0.5%.
2. **Best model: Logistic Regression** (AUC=0.813, and also the highest F1 at 0.503).
3. **Key takeaway**: the most complex models (random forest, SVM) **did not beat** the simplest logistic regression. This shows the patterns in this data are **mostly linear**, with no strong nonlinear/interaction effects. → **Logistic regression should be chosen**: equally accurate, yet the fastest and most interpretable.
4. **A low recall (~0.3–0.4) is normal**: at the default 0.5 threshold, the model is conservative. If the business wants to catch more potential churners, you can **lower the decision threshold** (flag "high risk" at a lower probability), trading some precision for higher recall.

## 6. Business Recommendations

Based on the churn drivers the model discovered:

1. **Monthly-pay & new members churn most easily** → drive them toward long-term (annual) contracts + strengthen onboarding/care during the early membership period.
2. **Low visit frequency = a churn warning sign** → send reminders and re-activation campaigns to members who haven't visited in a while, intervening before they cancel.
3. **Members who live far away & joined via promotions are high-risk** → send targeted retention offers.
4. **Encourage personal training, group classes, and extra spending** → the more a member invests, the "stickier" they are; use member perks to guide this.
